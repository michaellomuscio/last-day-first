# Learning-science evidence base and minimal student model

Prepared 2026-11-17 for the planning-tool simulation. Purpose: an honest illustration of well-established findings, not a scoring system.

## How to read this brief

**Verification labels used throughout**
- **[V]** I fetched or searched the source this session and the stated point appeared in it (primary text, or an authoritative summary of it).
- **[V-2nd]** Confirmed only via a secondary summary (news release, teaching site, abstract page). Treat numbers as approximate until checked against the paper.
- **[U]** Unverified: from my background knowledge only; not checked this session. Do not hard-code without checking.

Limits of this research session: several PDFs/pages blocked automated fetch (PNAS 403, PMC bot check, paywalled journals). Where I could only read abstracts or secondary summaries, I say so. **Nothing below is invented**; where I do not have a number I say so.

"Sources say" and "My inference" are separated. Section 14 onward is my synthesis, not established fact.

## Part A. Evidence base

### 1. Dunlosky et al. 2013 and Pashler et al. 2007

**Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques," Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4-58.**
URLs: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html (release); DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266 [U on DOI].

Sources say [V-2nd; tiers from search summary of the monograph]:
| Utility | Techniques |
|---|---|
| High | Practice testing; distributed practice |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation; self-explanation; interleaved practice |
| Low | Summarization; highlighting/underlining; keyword mnemonic; imagery for text learning; rereading |

- Ratings reflect generalisability across learners, materials, criterion tasks and conditions, not only whether a technique ever works. Low-rated techniques sometimes help certain students on certain tasks.
- [U] The monograph notes most evidence is from undergraduates and laboratory tasks, with comparatively little from authentic school classrooms or younger students; and that it rated utility, not classroom feasibility. Check before citing.

My inference: the tool can say "this plan contains no retrieval or spaced revisiting" with high confidence; it should not say "highlighting is harmful" (it is rated low utility, not negative).

**Pashler, Rohrer, Cepeda & Carpenter / Pashler et al. (2007), "Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning," IES Practice Guide NCER 2007-2004.**
URL: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf [V: text read].

Recommendations and the guide's own evidence levels [V]:
1. Space learning over time (review after a delay of several weeks to several months) - **Moderate**
2. Interleave worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises - **Moderate**
3. Combine graphics with verbal descriptions - **Moderate**
4. Connect and integrate abstract and concrete representations - **Moderate**
5. Use quizzing with active retrieval - 5a pre-questions to introduce a topic: **Low**; 5b quizzes to re-expose students to key content: **Strong**
6. Help students allocate study time efficiently - 6a delayed judgments of learning: **Low**; 6b tests/quizzes to identify what to learn: **Low**
7. Ask deep explanatory questions - **Strong**

Also [V]: the guide says spacing "overshooting" costs much less than too-short spacing ("it makes sense to be sure to have enough spacing, but it rarely makes sense to worry about having too much"). A cited classroom study: eighth-graders in US history, review at 1 week vs 16 weeks, tested 9 months later; the 16-week group scored almost 100 percent higher (the guide's description). The guide itself notes the spacing literature is mostly lab studies of isolated facts, with few studies of complex structured knowledge.

### 2. Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving

- **Spacing gap vs retention interval.** Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), Psychological Science 19(11), 1095-1102. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/ [V abstract; 1,350+ participants, gaps up to 3.5 months, tests up to a year later; best gap rises then falls with gap length, and the optimum depends on the retention interval]. Reported rule of thumb [V-2nd, I did not read the full paper]: optimal gap roughly 20% (could be higher, ~20-40%, at short delays) of the desired retention interval for delays of weeks, falling toward ~5% for a one-year delay. A blog summary gives 1 day for a 1-week test and 11 days for a 1-month test: **unverified, approximate**. Lab word-fact tasks with adults; the authors' own conclusion is that many educational practices are likely inefficient. Related review: Cepeda et al. 2006 (cited in the IES guide) [U beyond that].
- **Classroom retrieval practice.** Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott (2011), J. Exp. Psychol: Applied, sixth-grade social studies: quizzed content did better on chapter and semester exams [V-2nd, abstract-level]. McDaniel, Agarwal, Huelser, McDermott & Roediger (2011), J. Educ. Psychol., middle-school science, quiz frequency/placement [V citation only; results not retrieved - unverified]. Search page: https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-in-the-classroom-long-term-improvements-fr/
- **Interleaving in classrooms.** Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015), J. Educ. Psychol., 126 seventh graders: interleaved vs blocked maths practice, Cohen's d 0.42 (immediate) and 0.79 (delayed) [V-2nd]. Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2019/2020), 54 seventh-grade classes, authors conclude effective and feasible [V-2nd; effect size not retrieved]. Rohrer et al. 2014 Tampa study 72% vs 38% [V-2nd, from a blog summary]. Source lists: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ956134, https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2021/10/28-1 .
- Honest note: interleaving in classrooms is concentrated in maths and one research group; Dunlosky rates it only moderate. Retrieval and spacing carry the strongest evidence. Gains are shown on delayed tests; interleaved/spaced practice feels harder and can produce lower in-the-moment performance [U - standard "desirable difficulties" claim, not re-checked].

### 3. Rosenshine (2012), "Principles of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That All Teachers Should Know," American Educator 36(1).
URL: https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf [V: text read].

The ten principles (as I recall/read): (1) begin with a short review of previous learning; (2) present new material in small steps with practice after each step; (3) ask a large number of questions and check responses of all students; (4) provide models; (5) guide student practice; (6) check for student understanding; (7) obtain a high success rate; (8) provide scaffolds for difficult tasks; (9) require and monitor independent practice; (10) engage students in weekly and monthly review. [V for 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 headings; 4, 6, 8, 9, 10 headings from memory - minor risk.]

Figures Rosenshine states [V]:
- Success rate: in one fourth-grade maths study, **82%** of answers correct in the most successful teachers' classrooms vs **73%** for the least successful. "The research also suggests that the optimal success rate ... appears to be about **80 percent**."
- "The most successful teachers in these studies spent **more than half of the class time lecturing, demonstrating, and asking questions**."
- Maths study: most effective teachers spent **about 23 of 40 minutes** presenting/demonstrating/questioning/working examples; least effective only **11 minutes**; least effective asked only **nine questions** in 40 minutes.
- Daily review example: teachers trained to spend **eight minutes** daily on review (elementary maths; students scored higher).
- His evidence base is explicitly three sources: classroom observation of "master" teachers, cognitive science, and studies of cognitive supports. Much of it is correlational process-product research from 1970s-80s, often elementary maths/reading; it is a synthesis, not a meta-analysis. Mark all figures as **observational**, not causal.

### 4. Formative assessment and feedback

- **Black & Wiliam (1998)** "Assessment and classroom learning," Assessment in Education 5(1), and the Kappan summary "Inside the black box." Claimed effect sizes of roughly 0.4-0.7 [V-2nd]. 
- **Criticism** [V-2nd]: Bennett (2011), "Formative assessment: a critical review," Assessment in Education 18(1) (https://people.bath.ac.uk/edspd/Weblinks/MA_Ass/Resources/Using%20assessment%20formatively/Bennett%202011%20AiE.pdf) argues the term covers many different practices and the meta-analytic sources were mixed in quality, and that the range comes mainly from the popular Kappan article and relies heavily on Fuchs & Fuchs (1986). Kingston & Nash (2011) re-analysed and found a much smaller effect, reported as about 0.2 (range 0.20-0.30) with low overall study quality [V-2nd, via https://www.edweek.org/education/has-the-research-on-formative-assessment-been-oversold/2009/05 and a PARE paper]. Briggs et al. dispute Kingston & Nash's methods. Net: formative assessment likely helps, **magnitude is contested; do not use 0.4-0.7**.
- **Kluger & DeNisi (1996)**, Psychological Bulletin 119, 254-284: 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations, mean d = .41, but **over one third of feedback interventions decreased performance** [V-2nd].
- **Hattie & Timperley (2007)**, "The Power of Feedback," Review of Educational Research 77(1), 81-112. https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Hattie-and-Timperley-(2007) [V-2nd]. Three questions (where going, how going, where next); four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self); praise directed at the self is least effective. A teaching site quotes 0.65 (task/process) vs 0.15 (self) [V-2nd, **unverified in the original**]. Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), Frontiers in Psychology, 435 studies re-examination (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456): I could not retrieve its numbers [unverified]. The Hattie "effect size league table" method has been criticised (averaging dissimilar designs; I did not retrieve a specific critique this session - **[U]**).
- **Wiliam's five key strategies** [U - from memory, not fetched]: (1) clarify and share learning intentions and success criteria; (2) engineer effective discussions, tasks and questions that elicit evidence of learning; (3) provide feedback that moves learners forward; (4) activate students as instructional resources for one another; (5) activate students as owners of their own learning.

### 5. Cognitive load, worked examples, attention

- **Cognitive load theory / worked examples.** Sweller & Cooper (1985) and Sweller, van Merrienboer & Paas are the standard sources; Kalyuga et al. (2003) expertise reversal. **[U: not fetched this session].** What the IES guide says [V]: interleaving worked examples with problems is **Moderate** evidence (Recommendation 2); combining graphics with verbal descriptions **Moderate** (R3). Expected consequence (standard CLT claim, [U]): worked examples help novices but can become redundant or harmful for more expert learners (expertise reversal) - so fading is sensible.
- **Attention in lectures.** Wilson & Korn (2007), "Attention during lectures: beyond ten minutes," Teaching of Psychology 34(2), 85-89 [V-2nd, via summary https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/instructional-design/student-attention-spans]. They traced the "10-15 minute" claim to secondary sources and personal observation; the best-known observation study had serious flaws; evidence on attention is mixed (note-taking declines, but retention seemed stable in one study); they found **no direct evidence of a fixed 10-15 minute span** and did not conclude that engagement can be ignored. 
- Implication: do **not** hard-code an attention cliff at 10-15 minutes. Any attention variable must be a labelled assumption.

### 6. Active learning vs lecture; direct instruction; minimal guidance

- **Freeman et al. (2014)**, PNAS 111(23), 8410-8415 (https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1319030111; I could not load the page, numbers from abstract-level secondary reports [V-2nd]): 225 studies; exam scores +0.47 SD (158 studies); failure odds ratio 1.95 (67 studies); failure rates 21.8% active vs 33.8% lecture. **Limits**: undergraduate STEM only; "active learning" defined broadly (at least some class time on activities requiring students to think; includes clickers, group problem solving); comparison is against traditional lecture, not against well-designed explicit instruction with questioning; many quasi-experimental designs. Not evidence about grades 9-12.
- **Secondary-school equivalents**: I did not verify any equivalent meta-analysis this session. Unverified. Do not generalise Freeman.
- **Direct instruction.** Stockard, Wood, Coughlin & Khoury (2018), Review of Educational Research 88(4), 479-507, doi 10.3102/0034654317751919 (https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/9c5db747-52be-4bd8-8771-b05d0f7c0b01/full) [V-2nd]: 328 studies, 413 designs, ~4,000 effects, 1966-2016; effects "moderate to large" by conventional benchmarks; study characteristics not systematically related to effect size. **Caveats**: the authors are associated with the National Institute for Direct Instruction; this is the capitalised DI (Engelmann) curriculum family, not all "direct teaching". I did not find the pooled d or an independent critique. Project Follow Through [U: no source retrieved; my recollection is that DI performed best in the Abt evaluation, and that the evaluation was methodologically contested].
- **Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006)**, Educational Psychologist 41(2), 75-86 [V-2nd; https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf]: argues, from cognitive architecture, that minimally guided instruction is less effective and efficient than guided instruction, especially for novices. It is a position paper, not a meta-analysis, and was contested (Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007 [U]).
- Inference: "Lecture" in the tool should not equal "bad." Teacher explanation followed by frequent questioning and practice (Rosenshine) is well supported; the issue is passive stretches with no response requirement.

### 7. Prior knowledge and misconceptions

- National Research Council (2000), *How People Learn: Brain, Mind, Experience, and School* (expanded ed.), https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/9853/ [U: not fetched this session]. Three key findings as I recall them: (1) students come with preconceptions that must be engaged or they may fail to grasp new concepts or revert to preconceptions outside the classroom; (2) competence needs factual knowledge organised in a conceptual framework, to support retrieval and application; (3) metacognitive approaches help learners monitor and control learning. Conceptual change literature (Posner et al. 1982; Chi) [U].
- Rosenshine [V]: misconceptions are likelier when too much is presented at once and the teacher does not check understanding.
- Inference: misconceptions are persistent and not removed by exposure; they need elicitation and targeted feedback. Specific rates of persistence are not something I can cite.

### 8. Transfer

- Barnett & Ceci (2002), "When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer," Psychological Bulletin 128(4), 612-637. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12081085/ [V abstract]. They argue a single effect size for far transfer is misguided given the complexity; a taxonomy of content and context dimensions (nine; [V-2nd]).
- Perkins & Salomon (low road / high road; hugging and bridging) [V-2nd]. Perkins & Salomon 1988/1992 primary text not read.
- Inference: transfer to dissimilar contexts is unreliable; it needs varied contexts and explicit abstraction. The tool should model near transfer weakly and far transfer as unknown/low-default.

### 9. Homework (high school)

- **Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006)**, "Does homework improve academic achievement? A synthesis of research, 1987-2003," Review of Educational Research 76(1), 1-62. https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/688435 [V-2nd]. Duke release (https://today.duke.edu/2006/03/homework.html) [V]: correlation "much stronger for secondary students" (grades 7-12); "Even for high school students, overloading them with homework is not associated with higher grades"; for upper high school, "after about two hours' worth, more homework was not associated with higher achievement"; the 10-minute rule ("add 10 minutes of homework as students progress one grade" - Cooper's description of practice consistent with the research, i.e. a convention, not a finding).
- A research brief puts the high-school plateau at about two hours with optimum ~90 min-2.5 h [V-2nd]. Mostly **correlational** studies (the synthesis separates weaker correlational from random-assignment studies [U]); homework time confounds with ability and motivation.
- **Purpose**: practice vs preparation vs extension - Cooper's 1989 typology [U]; I did not retrieve evidence ranking these. The classroom guidance that homework is a spacing opportunity comes from the IES guide [V].
- **Typical load**: Galloway, Conner & Pope (2013): 4,317 students at ten high-performing Northern California schools, average 3.11 hours per weeknight, linked with stress and health problems [V-2nd]. Challenge Success: 2.8 h average, 3.2 h with an AP course [V-2nd]. A national benchmark (Pew/Gallup/NCES) **not found - unverified**. Do not present these as US averages: they are affluent, high-performing samples.
- **Sleep**: a survey of 1,004 US teens (2018): 79% got 7 hours or less on school nights [V-2nd, separate sample from the homework data]. Recommended 8-10 hours for teens (AASM/AAP) [U].

### 10. Cooperative / group work

- Slavin (1995) *Cooperative Learning* and Johnson & Johnson (2009): group goals plus individual accountability are the conditions under which achievement effects appear; Johnson & Johnson's five elements (positive interdependence, individual accountability, promotive interaction, social skills, group processing) [V-2nd; **primary texts not read**]. A review reports cooperative learning beat competitive (ES 0.54) and individualistic (0.51) learning [V-2nd, source unnamed on page; unreliable]. A secondary source says group goals without accountability showed weaker effects [low-reliability source; unverified].
- Inference: group work with no individual accountability should be flagged as high-risk for uneven participation, not as ineffective in general.

### 11. Motivation and engagement

- Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000) [U, not fetched]; expectancy-value (Eccles & Wigfield 2002) [U]. Rosenshine's ~80% success rate is a competence-support proxy [V]. Hattie & Timperley: self-level praise ineffective [V-2nd].
- I did not retrieve effect sizes; engagement should be modelled simply and flagged as assumption-heavy.

### 12. Opportunity to respond, wait time, participation

- Rosenshine [V]: more effective teachers asked many more questions (nine questions in 40 min for the least effective), checked all students via choral response, cards, tell-a-neighbour.
- Rowe (1974, 1986): average wait time about 0.9 s; extending to 3-5 s increased response length, unsolicited responses and student-student interaction and decreased non-response [V-2nd; practitioner sources]. Tobin (1980s): achievement on higher-cognitive items rose when wait time exceeded 3 s [V-2nd]. Rowe's primary papers not read; effect sizes not retrieved.
- **How many students answer in whole-class questioning**: I found **no verified figure**. Do not invent one; model it as a parameter set by activity tag (hand-raise vs cold call vs all-respond) and let the user see it as an assumption.

## Part B. Synthesis (my proposal, not established fact)

### 13. Design principle
The simulation should output **ranges and explicit assumptions**, run over a heterogeneous synthetic class (varying prior knowledge, attention, speed), and state "expected under these assumptions", never "students will learn X".

### 14. State variables (per student, per learning goal; scale 0-1 unless noted)
1. **Exposure (E)** - has the content been presented (count of exposures, time since last).
2. **Comprehension (U)** - initial understanding after instruction (depends on prior knowledge, load, checking).
3. **Retrieval strength (R)** - how retrievable now; decays with time, boosted by successful retrieval, with spacing-dependent gains.
4. **Practice count and success rate (P, S)** - number of responses made and fraction correct (feeds the 80% rule).
5. **Connected understanding (C)** - links to prior knowledge and across representations/contexts (explanation, comparison, worked examples).
6. **Misconception (M)** - probability that a wrong model is held, plus whether it has been surfaced.
7. **Fluency (F)** - automaticity of component skills (lowers working-memory load).
8. **Engagement/motivation (A)** - simple composite of competence (success), autonomy/relevance, relatedness, with short-run attention modifier.
Per student, not per goal: **Fatigue/load (L)** and **cumulative workload/sleep proxy**; time-use ledger by tag.

### 15. Rules (confidence: WE well established, MS moderately supported, PA plausible assumption)
| # | Rule | Source | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A successful retrieval event (quiz, cold-call, recall task) raises R more than the same time spent re-reading/restudying; effect larger on delayed than immediate tests. | Dunlosky (High); IES 5b (Strong); Roediger 2011 | WE |
| 2 | Retrieval without feedback helps less; if the response is wrong and uncorrected, M may rise. | Rosenshine; Kluger & DeNisi (feedback can hurt); IES | MS |
| 3 | Re-exposure after a gap gives more R gain than massed repetition of the same total time. | Dunlosky (High); IES R1 (Moderate); Cepeda 2008 | WE (direction) |
| 4 | Optimal gap scales with wanted retention: ~10-40% of the interval for retention of days-weeks, a smaller fraction for a year; too-short gaps cost much more than too-long. Parameterise, show as range. | Cepeda 2008 [V-2nd]; IES | MS |
| 5 | Interleaved practice raises delayed performance on problem-type discrimination (esp. maths), at the cost of lower in-session success and higher perceived difficulty. | Rohrer 2015; Dunlosky (Moderate) | MS |
| 6 | Presenting new material in small steps with a response after each step raises U; large chunks lower U and raise M risk. | Rosenshine P2; CLT [U] | MS |
| 7 | Practice success rate near 80% is the target band; below roughly 70%, practice raises M and lowers A; near 100% adds little challenge. Treat 80% as rough, observational. | Rosenshine (82 vs 73, ~80) [V] | MS (observational) |
| 8 | Guided practice with teacher checking precedes independent practice; independent practice without enough guided success produces error practice. | Rosenshine P5, P7, P9 | MS |
| 9 | Worked examples, then problem-solving, raise U/F for novices; fade as prior knowledge grows (expertise reversal). | IES R2 (Moderate); Sweller [U] | MS |
| 10 | Graphics plus verbal explanation help novices; mismatched/redundant text lowers U. | IES R3 (Moderate) | MS |
| 11 | Deep explanatory questions ("why", "how does this compare") raise C more than recall questions. | IES R7 (Strong); Dunlosky (self-explanation, elaborative interrogation: Moderate) | MS-WE |
| 12 | Task and process-level feedback on a specific response reduces M and raises S; praise-only feedback does not. Feedback has a positive average effect but a substantial minority of cases harm. | Hattie & Timperley; Kluger & DeNisi | MS |
| 13 | Prior knowledge scales gains: low prior knowledge means lower U, higher load, higher M risk; a diagnostic that surfaces M lets feedback target it. | NRC [U]; Rosenshine | MS |
| 14 | A passive stretch with no student response lowers A and attention; the penalty is modest and not tied to any fixed minute mark. A response opportunity restores it. | Wilson & Korn (no fixed span); Rowe | PA |
| 15 | Fraction of students making an active response depends on technique: all-respond (cards, mini-whiteboards, neighbour talk) near whole class, volunteer hand-raise small subset. Percentages are assumptions the user can edit. | Rosenshine examples; no verified rates | PA |
| 16 | Wait time under about 3 s reduces length and quality of responses; 3 s or more increases them. | Rowe; Tobin [V-2nd] | MS |
| 17 | Homework adds R/F gains if it is practice or spaced review at the right success level; benefit per added minute falls past about 2 h total per night for high school; heavy load lowers sleep and A. | Cooper 2006 [V]; Galloway 2013 | MS (benefit curve), PA (shape) |
| 18 | Total nightly load across all classes (the tool can only see one teacher's share) is shown as a range with a warning, not scored. | Galloway [V-2nd] | PA |
| 19 | Group work raises C and A only when it has group goals plus individual accountability; otherwise participation is uneven and M may go uncorrected. | Slavin; Johnson & Johnson [V-2nd] | MS |
| 20 | Far transfer is low by default; raised only by varied contexts and explicit bridging; near transfer to similar problems is higher. | Barnett & Ceci; Perkins & Salomon | MS |
| 21 | Minimally guided tasks with novices on new content give lower U and higher M than guided versions; with prior knowledge the gap narrows. | Kirschner 2006 | MS (contested) |
| 22 | Competence, autonomy/relevance and relatedness each nudge A upward; A modulates attention and homework completion. Size unknown. | SDT; expectancy-value [U] | PA |
| 23 | Retention decays between exposures (forgetting curve); the shape and rate are parameters, not facts about these students. | Cepeda 2008; IES | MS |

### 16. What the model must refuse to claim
- Individual student outcomes, or predicted grades or test scores.
- Effect sizes from the lab applied as classroom guarantees; Freeman's results as proof for secondary schools; Hattie/Black-Wiliam league-table numbers.
- A fixed attention span, or that lecture is ineffective, or that active learning is always better.
- Whether a lesson will be understood: the model cannot see the quality of the teacher's explanation, the task content, or the questions asked.
- Real success rates, participation rates, and homework completion for this class (assumptions only).
- Total workload across other classes, sleep, family circumstances, equity effects.
- Far transfer, creativity, long-term motivation, and learning outcomes beyond the planned unit.
- Whether a misconception exists in this class (only that the plan leaves such risk unaddressed).
- Precise spacing gaps: only ranges.

### 17. Findings most at risk of being overstated
1. Black & Wiliam/Hattie effect sizes (0.4-0.7; 0.65) and the claim that feedback is uniformly beneficial.
2. Freeman 2014 generalised beyond undergraduate STEM.
3. Rosenshine's 80% success and "23 of 40 minutes" as causal rules.
4. The 10-minute rule and "two hours" plateau as causal homework thresholds.
5. The 10-15 min attention claim (debunked).
6. Cepeda's percentage-of-interval gap rule from lab fact-learning.
7. DI meta-analysis authored by programme-linked authors.
8. Interleaving's maths-heavy, single-group evidence.

### 18. Could not verify this session
Dunlosky primary text (only tiers via summary); Wiliam's five strategies; Black & Wiliam primary; Bennett critique primary text; Hattie-Timperley numbers; Wisniewski 2020 numbers; Sweller worked-example and expertise-reversal primaries; NRC findings; Project Follow Through; Stockard pooled d; independent critique of Stockard; any secondary-school active-learning meta-analysis; Cooper 2006 specific effect values; Cooper's purpose typology; a national US homework-time figure; Cepeda's exact optimal-gap tables; Slavin and Johnson & Johnson primaries; McDaniel 2011 results; SDT and expectancy-value primaries; rates of students answering in whole-class questioning.
