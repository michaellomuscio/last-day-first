# Observation taxonomies for a minute-by-minute "what are students doing" report

Compiled 2026-11-17. Purpose: ground the activity categories of a lesson-planning tool for new US high school teachers in published observation instruments and frameworks.

**Conventions.** "SOURCE SAYS" = taken from the cited document. "INFERENCE" = my reading, not stated by the source. "UNVERIFIED" = I could not reach a primary source or could not confirm the detail. "VERIFIED (primary)" = I read the original document text in this session. Section 8 is my own synthesis and is **not a published instrument**.

---

## 1. COPUS (Classroom Observation Protocol for Undergraduate STEM)

**Primary sources**
- Protocol sheet with all codes (VERIFIED, primary): https://cwsei.ubc.ca/files/resources/tools/COPUS_protocol.pdf
- Tool page, training guide, detailed code descriptions: https://cwsei.ubc.ca/resources/tools/copus
- Paper: Smith, M.K., Jones, F.H.M., Gilbert, S.L., Wieman, C.E. (2013). *CBE Life Sciences Education* 12(4), 618-627. https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.13-08-0154 (PMC copy: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3828954/, not readable by me)
- Adapted from TDOP (Hora, Oleson, Ferrare; see section 5).

### Method (SOURCE SAYS, protocol sheet)
- "Put a check under all codes that happen anytime in each 2 minute time period (check multiple codes where appropriate). If no codes fit, choose 'O' (other) and explain in comments."
- Observers need "a short 1.5 hour training period".
- Optional third section, Student Engagement, rated L / M / H per interval: L = "Small fraction (10-20%) obviously engaged"; M = "Substantial fractions both clearly engaged and clearly not engaged"; H = "Large fraction of students (80+%) clearly engaged in class activity or listening to instructor." Alternative: count about 10 students near you and record how many appear engaged at each 2-minute interval.
- INFERENCE: because several codes can be checked in one interval, COPUS percentages are "share of intervals in which code appeared", and they sum to more than 100%. A planning tool that assigns one code per minute needs either a primary-code rule or an optional secondary code.

### Student codes ("1. Students are Doing") - 13 codes, exact wording
| Code | Wording |
|---|---|
| L | Listening to instructor/taking notes, etc. |
| Ind | Individual thinking/problem solving. Only mark when an instructor explicitly asks students to think about a clicker question or another question/problem on their own. |
| CG | Discuss clicker question in groups of 2 or more students |
| WG | Working in groups on worksheet activity |
| OG | Other assigned group activity, such as responding to instructor question |
| AnQ | Student answering a question posed by the instructor with rest of class listening |
| SQ | Student asks question |
| WC | Engaged in whole class discussion by offering explanations, opinion, judgment, etc. to whole class, often facilitated by instructor |
| Prd | Making a prediction about the outcome of demo or experiment |
| SP | Presentation by student(s) |
| TQ | Test or quiz |
| W | Waiting (instructor late, working on fixing AV problems, instructor otherwise occupied, etc.) |
| O | Other - explain in comments |

### Instructor codes ("2. Instructor is Doing") - 12 codes, exact wording
| Code | Wording |
|---|---|
| Lec | Lecturing (presenting content, deriving mathematical results, presenting a problem solution, etc.) |
| RtW | Real-time writing on board, doc. projector, etc. (often checked off along with Lec) |
| FUp | Follow-up/feedback on clicker question or activity to entire class |
| PQ | Posing non-clicker question to students (non-rhetorical) |
| CQ | Asking a clicker question (mark the entire time the instructor is using a clicker question, not just when first asked) |
| AnQ | Listening to and answering student questions with entire class listening |
| MG | Moving through class guiding ongoing student work during active learning task |
| 1o1 | One-on-one extended discussion with one or a few individuals, not paying attention to the rest of the class (can be along with MG or AnQ) |
| D/V | Showing or conducting a demo, experiment, simulation, video, or animation |
| Adm | Administration (assign homework, return tests, etc.) |
| W | Waiting when there is an opportunity for an instructor to be interacting with or observing/listening to student or group activities and the instructor is not doing so |
| O | Other - explain in comments |

Total: 13 + 12 = 25 codes (matches the "25 COPUS codes" figure in the Lund paper summary below).

### Published "profiles" (SOURCE: Lund et al. 2015)
- Source: *CBE Life Sciences Education* 14(2), doi 10.1187/cbe.14-10-0168. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4477734 (read via a summarising fetch, not a line-by-line reading). First-author and page details UNVERIFIED by me.
- Data: 269 class periods, 73 STEM faculty, 28 research-intensive institutions, COPUS plus RTOP. Percentages are "% of 2-minute intervals in which the code appeared." Clustering used 8 non-redundant codes; "listening" was excluded because it occurred in about 95% of intervals.
- Ten profiles in four styles. Figures reported in the text I could read:
  - Lecturing styles (Lecture with slides; Lecture at board; Transitional lecture): lecture above 80% of intervals in the first two; group work about 2%.
  - Socratic (at board / with slides): lecture above 80%, more frequent short instructor-student exchanges; group work about 10% or less.
  - Peer instruction (Limited PI x2, Extensive PI): group work in about a quarter of intervals in Limited PI.
  - Collaborative: "Student-centered PI" group work about 50%, students answering about 31%. "Collaborative learning (group work)": lecture about 26%, group work about 51%, instructor moving among students about 25%.
- The full per-code table (their Table 5) was an image I could not read. UNVERIFIED beyond the numbers above.
- Secondary corroboration: a UBC poster lists a "Lecture (with slides)" profile with lecture in about 94% of intervals (https://cwsei.ubc.ca/files/outcomes/EOY/Barker-etal_COPUS-CI_CWSEI-EOY2015.pdf; I saw this only as a search snippet).
- Another secondary description: "Didactic" is about 80% lecture; "Interactive lecture" adds student-centered strategies (https://peer.asee.org/32975.pdf, snippet only).

**Fit for high school (INFERENCE):** COPUS student codes are tied to lecture-hall practice (clicker questions, worksheets). Its useful ideas for us are (a) a small closed list of student behaviours, (b) an explicit **Waiting** code, (c) separating whole-class talk, group talk and individual thinking, (d) "Test or quiz" as its own code, (e) coding at a fixed interval.

## 2. Stallings classroom observation system ("Stallings snapshot")

**Primary / institutional sources**
- World Bank brief: https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/sief-trust-fund/brief/the-stallings-classroom-snapshot
- World Bank user guide: Molina, E. et al. *Conducting Classroom Observations: Analyzing Classroom Dynamics and Instructional Time* (2015/2017). Catalog pages: https://seal.matrade.gov.my/neuaxis-e/Record/okr-10986-22401/Description and https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/8f5b687f-e4fd-5e5b-9243-0e2f8da019fe/download (I did not read these in full; I confirmed existence via search results only).
- Policy brief with benchmarks (read in full): Inter-American Dialogue/PREAL, "How Effectively Do Teachers Manage Their Classrooms for Learning?" July 2014. https://www.thedialogue.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/FINAL-Time-on-Task-Brief-7-23-14-PDF.pdf. It cites Bruns & Luque (2014), *Great Teachers*, World Bank, Fig. 2.2 p.107, for the Latin America data.

### Method (SOURCE SAYS, World Bank page)
- "Ten observations are made in every class, at evenly spaced intervals", e.g. every 3 minutes in a 30-minute class or every 5 minutes in a 50-minute class.
- Each observation is a 15-second scan, starting with the teacher, plus a headcount of students visibly not engaged with the teacher.
- Everything is expressed "as a share of official class time", so observers are present before class starts and until the official end.
- Training: about 2.5 days (tablet version); about 5 days for the paper-grid version to reach "high (over 70%) rates of consistency" (World Bank page).

### Categories
- World Bank page: teacher activity categories are **Reading aloud; Demonstration/lecture; Discussion/question and answer; Practice and drill; Assignment/class work; Copying.** It states data cover use of time as **instruction, classroom management, or off-task**, and teacher ability to keep students engaged.
- PREAL brief (describing the adapted snapshot): records (1) whether the teacher is in instruction, class management or other actions (absent, engaged with others, uninvolved); (2) which pedagogical practice: "reading aloud, exposition/demonstration, discussion, practice/drills, cooperative projects, seatwork, or copying"; (3) which materials (none, textbook, notebook, blackboard, learning aids, ICT); (4) how many students are visibly engaged in the teacher-led activity and how many are visibly off-task ("looking out the window, doodling, chatting").
- Search-snippet description from the World Bank guide: "The first six activities count as instruction, and the bottom eight are classroom management or off-task" (so 14 activity codes in the tablet version). UNVERIFIED in the original guide; I did not see the code list itself.

### Benchmarks (SOURCE SAYS, PREAL 2014 brief; secondary for the underlying numbers)
- "well-prepared teachers in the United States spend an average of 85% of class time on instruction, with 50% of class time going to active instruction and no more than 35% to seatwork or copying. They spend only 15% of their time on classroom management, and students are off-task less than 6% of the time." The brief attributes this to Stallings and Knight. UNVERIFIED against the original Stallings/Knight publication. It is a US benchmark from older, mostly elementary-grade research (INFERENCE; the brief does not say grade level).
- Latin American average comparison: at most about 65% of time on instruction in the best country; active instruction about 36% of time; passive instruction such as copying and seatwork about 25%; every country lost at least 9% of class time to off-task; ICT used about 2% of class time.
- Top vs. bottom achievement schools (Honduras): 68% vs 46% of class time on instruction.
- The brief notes that half of "lost" time stems from absences, late arrival/early departure, or "conducting other business during class time" (quoting Bruns & Luque p.108).
- INFERENCE: the Stallings "85/15/<6" split is a useful anchor for a "learning time vs. overhead" roll-up, but "instruction" there includes teacher-led work and seatwork, so it is not a measure of cognitive quality.

## 3. ICAP (Chi & Wylie 2014)

**Primary source (VERIFIED, read full text):** Chi, M.T.H. & Wylie, R. (2014). The ICAP framework: Linking cognitive engagement to active learning outcomes. *Educational Psychologist* 49(4), 219-243. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823 ; PDF: https://csi.asu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/ChiWylie2014ICAP.pdf

### The four modes, defined by overt behaviour (SOURCE SAYS)
- **Passive** (receiving): "learners being oriented toward and receiving information from the instructional materials without overtly doing anything else related to learning." Example: "listening without taking notes."
- **Active** (manipulating): "some form of overt motoric action or physical manipulation is undertaken." Must cause focused attention; reading aloud an entire passage is "closer to passive than active because attention was not focused on some specific passages."
- **Constructive** (generating): learners "generate or produce additional externalized outputs or products beyond what was provided in the learning materials", containing "new ideas that go beyond the information given; otherwise such behaviors are merely active/manipulative."
- **Interactive** (dialoguing): dialogues meeting two criteria: "(a) both partners' utterances must be primarily constructive, and (b) a sufficient degree of turn taking must occur." Partners can be a peer, teacher, parent or responsive computer agent. Two students copying each other's solutions are only active.

### Table 1 examples (SOURCE, verbatim list items)
| Task | Passive | Active | Constructive | Interactive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Listening to a lecture | Listening without doing anything else but oriented toward instruction | Repeating or rehearsing; copying solution steps; taking verbatim notes | Reflecting out loud; drawing concept maps; asking questions | Defending and arguing a position in dyads or small group |
| Reading a text | Reading entire text passages silently/aloud without doing anything else | Underlining or highlighting; summarizing by copy-and-delete | Self-explaining; integrating across texts; taking notes in one's own words | Asking and answering comprehension questions with a partner |
| Observing a video | Watching the video without doing anything else | Manipulating the tape by pausing, playing, fast-forward, rewind | Explaining concepts in the video; comparing and contrasting to prior knowledge | Debating with a peer about the justifications; discussing similarities and differences |

Other constructive examples in the text: posing problems, comparing and contrasting cases, making plans, generating predictions, drawing analogies, constructing timelines, drawing a free-body diagram not already in the text. Active example: copying a diagram already given.

### Claimed ordering
- Hypothesis: **Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive** (I > C > A > P) in learning outcomes.

### Limits (SOURCE SAYS, Section 3 "Caveats") and criticisms
- Authors: the taxonomy is "a gross first cut"; predictions use only one aspect of a learning situation (what students do). Factors that can override: assessment measures (shallow recall items show no differences; "easy multiple-choice questions was the same for all levels"), the kind of knowledge/instructional goal, and learner characteristics/prior knowledge (the text names three override factors; I read the assessment one in detail).
- Authors: classification is by overt behaviour, so covert processing is invisible ("it is only more likely that the overt behaviors are an approximation"). Students listening to a lecture might be covertly self-explaining.
- Authors: "the knowledge-change processes associated with each mode of engagement are hypothetical. We have not carried out studies to verify" them.
- Authors: classification is "not always clear-cut"; an activity can be ambiguous between passive and active depending on focused attention.
- External: a search snippet reports an experimental study whose results contradicted the ordering (passive learners did best), and suggests task complexity and learner preparedness as boundary conditions. Paper not identified or read: UNVERIFIED (snippet from https://www.tojet.net/articles/v23i2/2322.pdf and others).
- INFERENCE: ICAP is "which mode is *intended and observable*", not "how well the activity is done". A tool can label a planned activity's mode only if it states the intended output (e.g., "write a summary in your own words" is constructive; "copy the summary" is active).

## 4. Laurillard's six learning types, ABC Learning Design, UCL Learning Designer

### Six learning types (Laurillard, *Teaching as a Design Science*, 2012; Conversational Framework)
Definitions as reproduced by University of Queensland ITaLI (secondary; reproduces Laurillard): https://itali.uq.edu.au/teaching-guidance/teaching-practices/designing-learning-activities
1. **Acquisition** - students take in ideas presented by teachers, e.g. lectures, podcasts, readings, demonstrations and videos.
2. **Inquiry** - students investigate on their own, examining and comparing sources and materials related to the course content (online research, data analysis, site visits).
3. **Discussion** - students put their own ideas and questions into words and respond to those of teachers and peers (class/group talks, forums, polls, think-pair-share).
4. **Practice** - students complete teacher-set tasks, adjust their approach toward the goal, and use feedback to improve (problem sets, quizzes, role-plays, games).
5. **Collaboration** - groups work together on a problem, negotiating ideas and practising skills jointly, ideally giving each other peer feedback (group work, project work, jigsaw).
6. **Production** - students create a teacher-specified output that is shared publicly, so motivation comes from the audience (blogs, models, audio/video, e-portfolios, concept maps, writing).

UCL's ABC page uses the same six but names type 2 **Investigation** ("explore actively, searching for and evaluating information") and ties **Production** to assessment ("essays, reports, designs or performances... often linked to assessment"): https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/digital-education/category/abc-learning-design . Learning Designer uses **Inquiry** (edutech wiki: https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Learning_designer_tool).

### ABC Learning Design (UCL)
- 90-minute, paper-based team workshop; teams build a "storyboard" from cards for each of the six types showing "the type and sequence of activities needed to meet the module's learning outcomes" (UCL blog above).
- That page does not describe a time or proportion chart. I believe the method includes a graph of the type mix, but I could not verify it. UNVERIFIED.

### Learning Designer (UCL, learningdesigner.org)
- UCL guide: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/learning-designer/guide/ (HTTP 403 for me; not read). Evaluation paper: Laurillard et al. 2018, *BJET*, https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/bjet.12697 (403; not read).
- Description from a Times Higher Education walkthrough page (secondary): https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/applying-conversational-framework-using-online-learning-design-tool
  - Each activity has a drop-down for learning type, plus duration in minutes, group size, whether the teacher is present, online or face-to-face, synchronous or asynchronous, and number of resources.
  - A **pie chart of learning types**, weighted by the time assigned to each type, updates as the design changes.
  - An **Analysis tab** also shows: online vs face-to-face share; **teacher presence** share of time; synchronous vs asynchronous; and **individual, group and whole-class** division of time.
- This is the closest published precedent for our roll-up report. INFERENCE: its dimensions (type mix, teacher present, individual/group/whole-class) map directly to the roll-up dimensions in section 9.
- Time totalling: "will add up the learning time as you work" (edutech wiki).
- A Cambridge page cites a balance of "active learning (60%), social learning (20%) and passive learning (20%)" as a framework ratio of Cambridge's own implementation; NOT a Learning Designer default and not verified (https://coe.cambridge.org/learning-design).

## 5. Other instruments that code student activity or engagement over time

### 5a. TDOP (Teaching Dimensions Observation Protocol)
- Source (VERIFIED, primary working paper): Hora, M.T. (2013). *Exploring the Use of the TDOP to Develop Fine-grained Measures of Interactive Teaching in Undergraduate Science Classrooms.* WCER Working Paper 2013-6. https://wcer.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/348/2026/08/WCER_WorkingPaper_Hora_2013.10.15.pdf ; protocol site http://tdop.wceruw.org/
- Method: presence/absence of codes in **2-minute intervals**; the full instrument has 46-47 codes in five categories (the working paper's reduced Table 1 lists 14 fewer). Categories: Teaching Methods, Pedagogical Moves, Teacher-Student Interactions, Cognitive Engagement, Instructional Technology.
- Student-facing codes in Table 1 (descriptions verbatim): **Small group work (SGW)** "Students form into groups of 2+"; **Desk work (DW)** "Students complete work alone at desk"; **Student novel question (SNQ)**, **Student comprehension question (SCQ)**, **Student response (SR)** "Student responds to instructor question"; **Student peer interactions (PI)** "Students interact with one another"; **Problem-solving (PS)** "Students are asked to actively solve a closed-ended problem with a known solution"; **Creating (CR)** "...an open-ended problem w/o a known solution"; **Connecting to real-world (CN)**. Instructor-side methods include Lecturing (L, LPV, LHV, LDEM), **Socratic lecture (SOC-L)**, Working through problems (WP), Multi-media (MM), **Assessment (A)** "Instructor gathers student learning data."
- Benchmark numbers from the study sample (58 math and science classes, university): lecturing with pre-made visuals in 64% of intervals; with hand-made visuals 27%; assessment 11%; small group work 11% (9% lower-division, 15% upper-division); problem solving 15%; display questions 36%.
- Note from search snippets: the default cognitive mode in observed classes was passive listening, so the cognitive-engagement codes only flag departures from it; and the developers state TDOP results are "only a measure of what is happening in the classroom and not a rating of it" (https://wcer.wisc.edu/?p=1510, snippet). Hora also built a taxonomy of active-learning modalities from TDOP codes called DOLA (Differentiated Overt Learning Activities), noted in the Chi & Wylie text too.

### 5b. IPI (Instructional Practices Inventory; Valentine, University of Missouri)
- Primary site: ipistudentengagement.com (not reachable by my tools). Category text below is from a Google Doc describing IPI-Technology, a secondary but detailed summary: https://docs.google.com/document/d/12ufN5xtULuTGoXU6fM2709-_HiPX7XYoY9rf7EC5AFw/export?format=txt . It states the IPI uses the same six categories and cites Valentine (2017).
- Six categories (names verbatim from that document):
  1. **Student Disengagement** - "not engaged in learning directly related to the curriculum."
  2. **Student Work with Teacher Not Engaged** - independent/group work building basic understanding, teacher not attentive (out of the room, at the computer, grading).
  3. **Student Work with Teacher Engaged** - same, teacher attentive/supportive; "practice, 'seatwork', worksheets, chapter review questions."
  4. **Teacher-Led Instruction** - "teacher dominated question/answer; teacher lecture or verbal explanations; teacher direction giving; and teacher demonstrations."
  5. **Student Verbal Learning Conversations** - higher-order thinking "driven by peer verbal interaction" (collaborative learning, peer tutoring, debate, Socratic learning).
  6. **Student Active Engaged Learning** - higher-order thinking "not driven by verbal interaction with peers" (project/problem-based learning, research/discovery, reflective journaling).
- The document says categories "are not considered a hierarchy but rather 'six distinct ways to categorize student engagement.'"
- Method (REL 2011-098 review): a ~3-minute observation per classroom, 100-120 classrooms in one day, results reported as percentage of observations in each category, for school improvement, "not for teacher evaluation." Source (VERIFIED, read): the report *Measuring student engagement in upper elementary through high school: a description of 21 instruments*, REL 2011-No. 098, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED514996.pdf (author list not verified from the file I read; the instrument table is what I used).
- Benchmark: "29.3 percent of observations in highly successful schools fell into the 'student active engaged learning' category, for example, versus 16.0 percent in unsuccessful schools" (Valentine 2007b, middle schools, via REL 2011-098). A search-snippet slide deck also lists "typical 15-25" and "highly effective 29.3" for category 6 (ACER, https://www.acer.edu.au/files/eppc15-Sabreena-Taylor.pdf; snippet only).
- Caution (REL report): observers who learned only from website materials had mean reliability under .20, versus over .90 after workshop training. INFERENCE: coding is judgment-heavy; our tool codes the *plan*, so it avoids observer reliability but gains a planning-intent problem instead.

### 5c. BOSS (Behavioral Observation of Students in Schools; Shapiro)
- Primary manual (Shapiro 2011) not reached. Secondary descriptions: Bahr 2012, *Computers in the Schools*, https://people.bath.ac.uk/edspd/Weblinks/MA_RME/Resources/Qualitative%20approaches/Bahr%202012%20CitS%20(observation).pdf ; REL 2011-098 above; reviews https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850679 .
- Categories (secondary): **Active Engaged Time** (e.g. writing, reading aloud, answering, raising a hand, talking about work) and **Passive Engaged Time** (e.g. listening to the teacher, reading silently, looking at the instructional material); **Off-Task Motor**, **Off-Task Verbal**, **Off-Task Passive**; plus a teacher-directed-instruction check. Interval recording of target students (15-second intervals per the REL summary). Reports "percentage of intervals engaged."
- REL 2011-098 describes a related instrument (MS-CISSAR/eBASS) with student behaviours **Academic responding** (writing, academic game, asking/answering, reading), **Task management** (e.g. raising hand for help, looking for materials), **Competing/inappropriate behavior.**
- No benchmark numbers verified for BOSS.
- INFERENCE: BOSS's "Task management" and "Passive off-task" ideas support separate codes for logistics and idle time.

### 5d. Academic Learning Time (Beginning Teacher Evaluation Study, BTES)
- Source (VERIFIED, read): Fisher, C., Marliave, R., Filby, N. (1979). Improving teaching by increasing "academic learning time." *Educational Leadership*, Oct 1979. https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/journals/ed_lead/el_197910_fisher.pdf
- Definition: "Academic Learning Time, defined as the amount of time a student spends engaged in an academic task that he/she performs with high success." Components: **allocated time** (upper limit), **engaged time** (portion of allocated time the student pays attention), **success rate**.
- Findings quoted: allocated math time in grade 2 ranged "from around 25 minutes per day... to around 60 minutes"; grade 5 reading from about 60 to about 140 minutes/day; engagement rates averaged "about 50 percent" in some classes and approached "90 percent" in others (the text is an overview and does not state a single average).
- Design is correlational (field study, 1972-78; see https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/7691/CoreTrustSeal). Elementary grades 2 and 5, not high school.
- INFERENCE: the plan-level analogue for our tool is "allocated minutes", with success rate as a prompt ("is this task achievable without help?"). Engagement rate cannot be planned.

## 6. Gradual release of responsibility (GRR)

- Origin: Pearson, P.D. & Gallagher, M. (1983). The instruction of reading comprehension. *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 8(3), 317-344 (citation as given on Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradual_release_of_responsibility; original not read).
- Four-phase version: Fisher, D. & Frey, N., *Better Learning Through Structured Teaching* (ASCD, 2008; 2nd ed. 2013; years vary by source). Book not read; the phase summary comes from Wikipedia and a search summary.
- Phases and who holds the thinking (Wikipedia summary, secondary):
  1. **Focus lesson ("I do it")** - teacher models; students listen, take notes, ask for clarification.
  2. **Guided instruction ("We do it")** - teacher supports, often in small needs-based groups; students take on more work.
  3. **Collaborative learning ("You do it together")** - students work in small groups, helping one another; each still accountable for an individual product.
  4. **Independent learning ("You do it alone")** - students apply on their own; teacher observes and circulates.
- Fisher and Frey describe it as recursive, not strictly linear (search summary; UNVERIFIED in the book).
- INFERENCE: GRR labels the **allocation of responsibility**, not the student's activity. Same activity (e.g., solving a problem) can be Guided or Independent depending on teacher support. So it works as a *tag on a segment*, not as an activity category.

## 7. Time lost to transitions, attendance, setup and cleanup in secondary classes

I found **no credible published figure for planned transitions, setup and cleanup as a share of high school class time.** What exists is on interruptions and lost time:

- **Kraft & Monti-Nussbaum (2021), "The Big Problem With Little Interruptions to Classroom Learning,"** *AERA Open* 7(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584211028856 (VERIFIED, read). Providence, RI: 63 observed class periods in 5 high schools (average class 66 minutes), plus a district survey.
  - Observed external interruptions lasted on average 41 seconds, with subsequent disruptions 57 seconds on average; about 50% of interruptions caused a disruption; about 71 seconds lost per interruption and possible disruption (78 seconds in the observed high schools).
  - Observed time lost: about 3.3 minutes per hour in the observed high schools (2.2 excluding tardy students), roughly 9.9 days per year (6.7 excluding tardy), by my reading of the extracted Table 4 (column alignment inferred).
  - Teachers reported 6.9 minutes lost per hour vs. 4.5 for principals (text, verified).
  - Tardy and returning students were 38% of observed external interruptions.
  - Abstract: interruptions plus disruptions cost "between 10 and 20 days of instructional time" per year.
  - Limitation: these are *unplanned* interruptions (intercom, visitors, tardies), not routine transitions.
- **Rogers & Mirra (2014), *It's About Time*,** UCLA IDEA, California high schools survey. Executive summary (VERIFIED, read): https://idea.gseis.ucla.edu/projects/its-about-time/IAT%20Executive%20Summary.pdf/at_download/file . Over an average day, students in high-poverty schools lose "roughly 30 minutes more" than low-poverty schools to interruptions, delays or routines; year-level lost days 22.3 vs 12.6. A per-period figure widely reported (e.g. 18 vs 13 minutes) I could not confirm in the summary I read: UNVERIFIED. These are teacher-survey estimates, not observation.
- **PREAL/Stallings data (section 2):** at least 9% of class time lost to off-task in every Latin American country studied; about half of that from absences, late arrival/early departure or "other business." Not US high school.
- A secondary-source claim (Gilman & Knoll 1984, up to 60% of the secondary day consumed by non-instructional events) is UNVERIFIED and refers to the whole school day, not class periods.

**INFERENCE for the tool:** A defensible default is to reserve explicit minutes for Transition/Setup/Cleanup and let teachers see them as overhead. A sensible single warning figure from the verified data is "unplanned interruptions took about 3 minutes of each hour in Providence high schools," but there is no published default for planned transition length; leave that to the teacher with a prompt.

## 8. SYNTHESIS (mine, not a published instrument): merged taxonomy of 18 student-activity codes

Design rules I applied:
- Each code is an **observable student behaviour** (COPUS/ICAP style), not a teacher method.
- Splits follow distinctions the published instruments repeatedly make: receiving vs. producing (ICAP, Laurillard), alone vs. pair/group vs. whole class (COPUS, TDOP, Learning Designer), practice vs. creating (TDOP PS vs CR; Laurillard Practice vs Production), assessment as its own code (COPUS TQ, TDOP A), waiting and administration as codes (COPUS W, Adm; Stallings classroom management).
- ICAP mode is the **usual** mode when the task is designed as intended; the teacher's stated output can change it (see section 3 inference). Laurillard has no assessment or logistics type, so those are marked "none."

| # | Code (plain name) | Observable definition | Usual ICAP mode | Laurillard type | Lineage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Listening to teacher | Students are oriented toward the teacher who is explaining, modeling or giving directions; hands free of other work | Passive | Acquisition | COPUS L; Stallings lecture; IPI 4 |
| 2 | Watching a demo or video | Students watch a demonstration, video, simulation or film without being asked to produce anything | Passive | Acquisition | COPUS D/V; TDOP MM; ICAP |
| 3 | Reading | Students read text silently or aloud with no accompanying task | Passive | Acquisition | ICAP Table 1; Stallings reading |
| 4 | Copying or taking notes | Students copy from the board, copy solution steps, or take notes close to the source wording | Active | Acquisition | COPUS L ("taking notes"); ICAP active; Stallings copying |
| 5 | Practicing alone | Students individually work problems, drills, worksheets or exercises with a known right approach | Active (Constructive if explaining or inventing) | Practice | TDOP DW, PS; Stallings practice/seatwork; IPI 2-3 |
| 6 | Making something alone | Students individually generate a new product: writing in own words, drawing, composing, designing, coding, building | Constructive | Production | ICAP constructive; TDOP CR; studio/arts |
| 7 | Hands-on lab or investigation | Students run an experiment, collect or analyze data, or explore sources following a procedure or question | Active (Constructive when predicting, analyzing, drawing conclusions) | Inquiry (Practice for procedure drills) | COPUS Prd; Laurillard inquiry |
| 8 | Physical or performance skill practice | Students repeat a motor or performance skill (PE drill, instrument, lines, lab technique) with feedback | Active | Practice | Laurillard practice; ICAP active |
| 9 | Speaking practice with a partner | Students take turns producing target-language or rehearsed speech with a partner or in a small group | Interactive when both generate new utterances; Active when scripted | Practice (Discussion if open-ended) | Laurillard practice/discussion; ICAP interactive criterion |
| 10 | Answering or asking in whole-class Q&A | Individual students answer or ask a question while the rest listen; teacher leads the turn-taking | Active/Constructive for the speaker, Passive for listeners | Discussion | COPUS AnQ, SQ; TDOP SR; IPI 4 |
| 11 | Whole-class or seminar discussion | Many students speak to each other (not only to the teacher) with sustained turn-taking | Interactive | Discussion | COPUS WC; IPI 5 |
| 12 | Pair or small-group talk | Students discuss a question, compare answers or explain to each other in pairs or small groups (think-pair-share, peer instruction) | Interactive | Discussion | COPUS CG; TDOP PI; IPI 5 |
| 13 | Group project work | Small groups work together toward a shared product or problem, dividing and combining work | Interactive | Collaboration | COPUS WG/OG; Laurillard collaboration |
| 14 | Presenting or performing | One or more students present, perform or demonstrate to an audience | Constructive (presenter); Passive (audience) | Production | COPUS SP; Laurillard production |
| 15 | Being assessed | Students complete a test, quiz or graded performance under assessment conditions | None (outside ICAP; behaviour is closest to Constructive or Active) | None (nearest: Practice or Production) | COPUS TQ; TDOP A |
| 16 | Reviewing feedback and revising | Students read returned work or comments, self- or peer-assess, reflect, and revise | Constructive | Practice (feedback loop) | Laurillard practice; ICAP "reflecting and monitoring" |
| 17 | Transition, setup, cleanup, admin | Students move rooms or groups, get or put away materials, take attendance, or follow logistics instructions | None | None | COPUS Adm; Stallings classroom management; BOSS task management |
| 18 | Waiting or unoccupied | Students have nothing assigned (waiting for teacher, materials, tech, or others) | None | None | COPUS W |

Notes:
- Off-task behaviour is deliberately **not** a planned code; it is observable only in practice (Stallings/BOSS/IPI category 1). The tool can show "minutes at risk of becoming off-task" (code 18 and long 1-3 runs) instead.
- The tool should allow an optional **secondary code** per minute (COPUS allows several per interval). Example: "Listening to teacher" + "Copying or taking notes".
- Codes 7-9 and 6 cover the subject-specific cases requested (lab, studio/arts, world-language speaking, PE).
- This list has not been tested for inter-rater reliability. Treat as a hypothesis to pilot with real teachers.

## 9. SYNTHESIS: roll-up dimensions for the summary report

1. **Receiving vs. doing vs. producing vs. dialoguing (ICAP mix).** Share of minutes by Passive / Active / Constructive / Interactive, with "none" shown separately. Source ideas: ICAP; Learning Designer-style pie chart. Show as a stacked bar, not a score (ICAP is a prediction about intended mode, not a quality rating).
2. **Alone vs. with a partner or group vs. whole class.** Minutes by social arrangement. Lineage: Learning Designer "individual, group, whole class"; COPUS group codes.
3. **Who is doing the thinking.** Tag each segment with a gradual-release phase (Teacher models / Guided / Together / Independent). Shows the arc across the lesson; flag lessons that go from Focus straight to Independent with no Guided or Together segment (INFERENCE: this is a common planning gap, no published benchmark).
4. **Learning-type mix (Laurillard six).** Minutes per type; pie as in Learning Designer. Include "none" (assessment, logistics, waiting) as its own wedge.
5. **Learning time vs. overhead.** Instruction-type minutes vs. Transition/Admin vs. Waiting vs. Assessment. Comparators with sources: Stallings 85% instruction / 15% management (US reference per a secondary brief); Providence about 3 minutes lost per hour to unplanned interruptions (Kraft & Monti-Nussbaum 2021).
6. **Student talk and longest passive run (two small indicators).** (a) Minutes in which students are speaking (codes 9-14) vs. silent. (b) Longest unbroken run of Passive-mode minutes. INFERENCE: both are derivable from the same codes and echo COPUS interval logic; **no published threshold** exists for either, so show the value and a neutral label, not a pass/fail.

Reference comparison ranges to show alongside (all with caveats from the source sections): COPUS lecture profiles (lecture above 80% of intervals), TDOP small-group in 9-15% of intervals, IPI category 6 about 16% to 29% of observations (middle schools), Stallings no more than 35% seatwork/copying and under 6% off-task.
